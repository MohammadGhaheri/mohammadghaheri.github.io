When I designed my **Business Intelligence with Power BI** course, the goal was never to teach Power BI as a collection of menus, charts and isolated features.

You can learn how to import an Excel file, build a few visuals or write a few measures relatively quickly.

The real challenge starts when you have to work with actual business data:

**How do you turn raw data into a sound analytical model and then into a report that people can genuinely use to make decisions?**

That question shaped the course I created for Maktabkhooneh. Today, with more than **18,000 learners**, it has become one of the most widely attended Persian-language Power BI courses.

## Built around Microsoft PL-300

The core structure of the course is based on the skills covered by **Microsoft PL-300: Power BI Data Analyst**.

Instead of teaching disconnected features, the learning path follows the actual analytical workflow:

- acquiring and preparing data
- cleaning and transforming data with Power Query
- designing a data model
- creating relationships between tables
- analysis and calculations with DAX
- report design and visualization
- extracting insights from data
- publishing and using Power BI in an organizational environment

The intention is not only to teach students how to use Power BI, but also to help them understand the workflow and reasoning of a data analyst.

## Why PL-300 alone was not enough for Iranian businesses

Simply translating Microsoft's standard learning path would have left out several problems that Iranian analysts routinely face.

A straightforward example is the **Persian calendar**.

Sales, finance, HR and operational reporting in many Iranian organizations needs to work with Solar Hijri years, months and dates.

I therefore added dedicated material for Persian-calendar analysis, date modeling and other local reporting requirements.

Localization goes beyond dates.

The examples, exercises and project scenarios are designed to resemble problems analysts actually encounter inside businesses rather than datasets created only to demonstrate a software feature.

## From data cleaning to data modeling

One of the most common mistakes I see when people learn Power BI is moving to charts too early.

In a real project, the quality of a dashboard is often determined before the first chart is built.

If the data is poorly cleaned, the model is incorrect or relationships between tables are badly designed, even a visually impressive dashboard cannot produce trustworthy analysis.

That is why a significant part of the course focuses on data acquisition, transformation, modeling and proper table relationships.

## DAX: where the analysis becomes more serious

Once the model is in place, one of the most important parts of Power BI begins:

**DAX**

I did not want DAX to be taught as a list of functions to memorize.

Understanding **context** and calculation logic is far more important.

Once that logic is clear, learners can build analyses such as cumulative sales, period comparisons, growth, product or branch contribution, management KPIs and time-based analysis.

This is where Power BI starts moving from a reporting tool toward a genuine business-analysis platform.

## Practical projects, not just end-of-chapter exercises

The complementary projects were an important part of the course from the beginning.

An exercise is usually designed to test one concept.

A real project is different.

The data is rarely clean. Business requirements may be incomplete. Multiple data sources may be involved. KPIs must be defined, a model must be designed, and the final report has to be useful to an actual manager or business user.

The course projects are therefore designed to make learners combine multiple skills instead of repeating a single Power BI feature.
